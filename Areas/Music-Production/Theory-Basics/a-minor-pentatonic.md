# A Minor and the Pentatonic Shortcut

When a track is **in a key**, that key gives us a small set of notes that "belong" together — they sound resolved, intentional, musical. Notes *outside* the key tend to sound wrong (clashing, accidental). For [[../Track-Sketches/techno-sketch-01|Techno Sketch 01]] we chose **A minor**, so the first useful theory bit is: *which notes are in A minor*, and the even shorter list of notes that *always* sound right.

## What "in a key" means in plain words

A **key** = a chosen home note + a chosen scale (a pattern of which notes you use).

Think of it like a vocabulary list for the track. Words on the list = safe. Words not on the list = risky (sometimes great for flavor, often just wrong-sounding).

The **home note** is the one your ear keeps wanting to return to — it sounds resolved, "at rest". Everything else in the scale creates motion away from home; landing on the home note creates the feeling of arriving.

For us: **home note = A**, **scale = minor**.

## A minor — the full scale (7 notes)

A minor uses **all the white keys** on a piano, starting from A:

```
A  B  C  D  E  F  G  (A again, an octave up)
1  2  ♭3 4  5  ♭6 ♭7
```

No sharps, no flats — which is why it's the easiest minor key to think in. (Its "relative major" is C major, which also uses all white keys but starts on C. Different home note, different feel — same notes.)

**Why "minor" sounds darker than major:** the difference between major and minor comes down to a few notes being a half-step lower in minor. Specifically the **3rd**, the **6th**, and the **7th** — those are the ♭ (flat) notes in the diagram above. The lowered 3rd in particular is what your ear hears as "sad / dark / serious".

## The pentatonic shortcut (5 notes — totally safe)

**Pentatonic** = "five notes". The **A minor pentatonic scale** drops 2 notes from the full minor scale — the 2 most "risky" ones:

```
Full A minor:        A B C D E F G
A minor pentatonic:  A   C D E   G   ← drop B and F
```

Why drop those two? **B and F** are the most likely to clash with each other and with the chord under them. Removing them leaves only notes that sound consonant against an A-minor backdrop, in any combination, in any order.

This is the **beginner cheat code**: when in doubt, restrict yourself to pentatonic notes. You can play them in any rhythm, any octave, any order — it will sound *musical*. It might not sound *interesting*, but it won't sound *wrong*.

## How this looks on the keyboard

```
White keys:   A   B   C   D   E   F   G   A
Pentatonic:   ✓       ✓   ✓   ✓       ✓   ✓
```

In Ableton's MIDI editor, the piano keyboard runs vertically on the left. To stay safe in A minor pentatonic, only draw notes on the **A, C, D, E, G** rows. Ignore B and F.

## The A minor triad — even safer

A **triad** = 3 notes stacked, the building block of a chord. The A minor triad is:

```
A  +  C  +  E
1     ♭3    5
```

That's the **most safe** subset of all — root, minor third, fifth. Any motif using only A, C, E will sound unmistakably "in A minor", resolved, and musical. The first lead in our sketch uses exactly this: A, C, and E.

## Octaves and registers

The same note name (A) repeats every octave: **A0, A1, A2, A3, A4...** — same pitch class, different height. In our sketch:

| Element | Octave | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bass | A1 | Deep, "bass" register |
| Lead | A2 / A3 | Higher, sits *above* the bass, easy to hear over the drums |

You can write the same notes (A, C, D, E, G) at any octave — the role they play in the track is set by the octave you put them in.
