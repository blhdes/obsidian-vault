# Sevenths and Ninths

Chords are built by **stacking every other note of the scale** on top of a root. The numbers in a chord name say how far up the scale you stacked.

In A minor (A B C D E F G, numbered 1–7):

| Chord | Formula | Notes | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Am** (triad) | 1-3-5 | A C E | Plain, resolved — the known safe chord |
| **Am7** | 1-3-5-**7** | A C E **G** | Softer, rounder, first hint of jazz |
| **Am9** | 1-3-5-7-**9** | A C E G **B** | Lush, colored, floating — *the Detroit chord* |

## Why "9" and not "2"?

B *is* step 2 of the scale — but chord numbering keeps counting past the octave: 8 = the root again, so the 2nd *above* the octave = **9**. Same note name, but voiced an octave up so it colors the chord instead of crowding the root.

## The every-other-letter pattern

Stack thirds and the letters skip alternately: **A → C → E → G → B**. Each added layer keeps more of the scale ringing at once — that's literally all "extensions" are. More layers = more color and less plain-ness; the triad states the key, the 9th *floats* in it.

Genre note: extensions are universal (jazz, soul, house, pop). Detroit techno's signature is specifically the **minor 7th/9th played as a short stab** — see [[../Techniques/detroit-chord-stab|Detroit Chord Stab]].
