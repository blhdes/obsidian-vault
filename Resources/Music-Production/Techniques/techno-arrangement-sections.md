# Techno Arrangement — Intro / Build / Drop / Break

A finished techno track isn't 4 minutes of the same loop — it's the same loop shown to the listener in **different combinations**, in a sequence that creates **tension and release**. Each "section" is just *which instruments are playing right now*. Strip an instrument away → tension. Bring it back → release.

This is genre-agnostic at heart (rock songs have verse/chorus, jazz has heads/solos), but techno has its own conventions worth knowing.

## The four classic sections

### 1. Intro
- **What plays:** Drums only (often kick + hats, no clap yet).
- **Purpose:** Establish the tempo and pulse. Give the listener something to grab onto.
- **Why it works:** Sparseness creates anticipation — your ear knows "something is missing" and waits for it.
- **Length in a club track:** 16–32 bars typically (long, by design — it's there for the DJ to mix in over).

### 2. Build (or Pre-Drop)
- **What plays:** Drums + bass come together. Maybe a filtered version of the lead (cutoff still low).
- **Purpose:** Layer the low end. The track now has weight but not yet melody.
- **Signature move:** This is where a **filter sweep** on the lead lives — cutoff slowly opening, leading the ear toward...

### 3. Drop
- **What plays:** *Everything.* Full kit, full bass, full lead, filter wide open.
- **Purpose:** Maximum energy. This is the section the track has been promising.
- **Why it works:** All the elements finally locked in together. The listener was waiting; here it is.

### 4. Break
- **What plays:** **Remove the kick** (and often the bass too). Keep hats, lead, percussion.
- **Purpose:** Pull the floor out. The pulse you've been dancing to *disappears* — your body keeps moving but the foundation is gone.
- **Why it works:** When the kick **returns** (back to the Drop), it lands like an impact — that re-entry is the most powerful moment in the track. The break exists *to set up the kick's return*.

### Bonus: Outro
- **What plays:** Mirror of the Intro — strip elements away, end on drums (or just kick).
- **Purpose:** Give the next DJ something to mix into. End softly, not on full energy.

## How this maps to Ableton scenes (Session View)

Each section = **one scene** = a horizontal row in Session View where you choose which clip plays on which track. **Empty cell = stop signal** for that track.

For the simplest 3-scene version:

|              | Intro | Drop | Break |
|--------------|-------|------|-------|
| **Drums**    | clip  | clip | clip  |
| **Bass**     | —     | clip | clip  |
| **Lead**     | —     | clip | —     |

For a 4-scene mini-arrangement:

|              | Intro | Build | Drop | Break |
|--------------|-------|-------|------|-------|
| **Drums**    | clip  | clip  | clip | clip  |
| **Bass**     | —     | clip  | clip | clip  |
| **Lead**     | —     | —     | clip | —     |

The cells with `—` are *empty* in that scene → when that scene launches, those tracks stop. Cells with `clip` continue / start.

## Variation techniques (when you want fancier)

- **Different drum clips for different sections.** A "drums + hats only" clip for the Intro, a "full drums" clip for the Drop. Removing the kick alone (rather than all drums) requires this — there's no per-note mute at the scene level.
- **Different bass clips.** A sparser bassline for the Break.
- **Filter envelope variants.** Different lead clips with different cutoff sweep curves per scene.
- **Length variants.** A 4-bar drum clip for the Intro (faster cycle, more urgency) vs. an 8-bar clip for the Drop.

For a first sketch, keep it to one clip per part and reuse them across scenes via the empty-cell rule. Fancier later.

## The "kick drop" is sacred

In dance music, **removing and re-adding the kick** is the single most reliable emotional move you have. The break removes it. The drop re-introduces it. Audiences respond to this every time, in every style, at every BPM. Use it.
