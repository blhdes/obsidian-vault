# Soul → Techno transitions

A "how to get from X to Y" recipe for bridging **1960s soul** (warm, live-played, vinyl-era) into **modern 4/4 techno** (cold, machine-made, electronic). They're decades and worlds apart, which is exactly what makes the move feel dramatic when it lands. This note is built around a real worked example from the library — the two sides of the **Johnnie Mae Matthews** 45 — so I have a concrete sample to copy next time.

**The core difficulty:** soul records from this era sit around **90–115 BPM** (*BPM = beats per minute, the speed of the track*), played by a real drummer so the tempo even drifts slightly. Techno sits around **125–135 BPM** and is machine-locked. That gap is too wide to "beatmatch" — *beatmatching = nudging two tracks to the exact same speed so their kicks line up and you can blend them.* The SC LIVE 4's pitch fader only stretches a track by about ±8% before it sounds warped, and stretching a live-drummer recording that far sounds awful anyway. So **don't try to blend them gradually.** Use a hard transition instead.

---

## The two techniques

### 1. The slam (easiest, and a genuine classic)

Play the soul track as a full *moment* — let people hear the vocal, enjoy it as a song, not just a tool. Meanwhile set a **hot cue** (*a saved marker you can jump to instantly*) on the **first kick drum of the techno track**. The instant the soul track hits its last note, **cut straight over** to the techno cue. No blend, no fade — a clean jump.

The whole point is the contrast: warm human voice → cold machine pulse. Done on the beat, it reads as intentional and hits hard. Done sloppily it just sounds like the song ended — so the timing of that cut is the only thing that matters.

### 2. The echo-out (smoother, hides the tempo gap entirely)

1. Near the end of the soul track, catch a short **loop** of the vocal (*loop = a few beats set to repeat).*
2. Push the **echo / delay FX** on it (*echo repeats the sound in fading copies; on the SC LIVE 4 this is an FX pad — see the cheatsheet linked below*).
3. **Kill the channel fader** so the dry track stops, but let the echo **tail ring out** — the vocal dissolves into a cloud of fading repeats.
4. Under that cloud, **fade up the techno track's intro** at its own tempo.

Because the echo smears the soul track into atmosphere, there's no longer a clear beat to clash with the techno — the tempo gap just disappears into the haze. This is the safer move on a big system where a hard slam can feel jarring.

## Worked example — the Johnnie Mae Matthews 45

The library has both sides of her 1969 **Big Hit Records** 7" single (Discogs 4979877), filed under `Johnnie Mae Matthews/Singles/`. They behave very differently as mix material:

| Track | Rough BPM | Mixable? | Use it as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I Have No Choice** (A-side) | ~110, steady stomper | **Yes** | the soul track in either technique above |
| **That's When It Hurts** (B-side) | ~87, slow ballad | No | a set **opener or closer**, played alone for mood |

> The B-side is a slow ballad with no driving beat — there's nothing to slam *out of* cleanly, so don't force it into a techno transition. Save it for the very start or end of a set as a standalone moment.

**The recommended pairing:** slam **I Have No Choice** straight into **[[Areas/Mixing-DJing/Library/june-2026|LoSoul — Open Door (Theo Parrish "Behind Closed Doors" Remix)]]**. Theo Parrish is a Detroit producer who builds his techno *out of* old soul and disco records, so the handoff sounds like it belongs together rather than random — the perfect first soul→techno experiment.

### Other techno partners in the library
- **LoSoul — Open Door (Theo Parrish remix)** — top pick (above); soulful-rooted techno, the most natural landing.
- **[[dub-techno|Basic Channel — Phylyps Trak]]** — slow, hypnotic [[dub-techno]] with a long, forgiving intro. Ideal target for the **echo-out**, since its foggy texture swallows the incoming vocal cloud.
- **Charlotte de Witte / Age of Love** — only as a **slam**, and only if I want maximum shock: warm 1969 soul straight into cold, dark peak-time techno. High-risk, high-reward.
- *Skip for now:* DJ Rush — too fast and aggressive, the tempo gap is even wider.

## General rule of thumb

When two tracks are too far apart in tempo or feel to beatmatch, **stop trying to blend and start thinking in cuts and FX.** A confident hard cut on the beat, or an echo-out into a fade, will always beat a warped pitch-stretch. The bigger the contrast between the two genres, the *more* a clean transition pays off — the surprise is the reward.

**First practice step:** import both sides into Engine DJ for real BPM/key analysis (Engine measures it on import — these aubio numbers are just estimates), then rehearse the slam from *I Have No Choice* into the Theo Parrish remix until the cut lands exactly on the techno track's first kick.
