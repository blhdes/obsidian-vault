# Garment Printing Techniques — Reference Paper

Companion to [[Projects/Blhades/production-plan|Production Plan]]. Every printing method that came up while sourcing producers for the White Canvas collection, in one place: how each works, where it wins, where it fails, and what it means for BLH*DES. External links go to Wikipedia.

---

## 1. Screen printing (serigrafía)

**How it works.** A fine mesh screen is coated with a light-sensitive emulsion; the design is burned into it so ink can only pass through the open areas. Ink is then pushed across the screen with a squeegee, one screen per colour, directly onto the fabric. Ancient technique, industrialised for textiles in the 20th century. → [Screen printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_printing)

**Strengths.** Thickest, most opaque ink deposit of any method — the only one where white-on-black looks truly solid. Most durable through washing. Largest print sizes. The slightly raised ink hand is part of the "premium tee" feel. Unlocks specialty inks (see §1a).

**Weaknesses.** Each colour needs its own screen (setup cost per design), so photographs and gradients get expensive — they must be simulated with [halftone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halftone) dots. Uneconomical for one-offs; shines from ~10+ units.

**For BLH*DES.** The chosen method. A monochrome, type-driven collection is mostly 1 screen per design = cheapest *and* highest quality simultaneously. The premium-streetwear standard.

### 1a. Screen-printing ink systems
- **[Plastisol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastisol)** — PVC-based ink that sits on top of the fabric. Maximum opacity and pop; slightly plasticky hand.
- **Water-based** — soaks into the fibre; soft, almost no hand; subtler on darks. What eco-leaning studios (TPOP, Wrong Culture-type shops) favour.
- **[Discharge printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discharge_printing)** — instead of adding pigment, the ink *removes* the garment's dye and replaces it. Ultra-soft prints on dark fabric; vintage feel.
- **Puff / high-density** — additives that raise the ink into 3-D texture. Trend-relevant for streetwear; ask studios about it later.
- **[Flocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flocking_(texture))** — adhesive print dusted with fibres for a velvet surface.

## 2. DTG — Direct-to-garment

**How it works.** Effectively an inkjet printer for fabric: water-based inks sprayed directly onto the garment, usually over a pre-treatment layer, then heat-cured. → [Direct-to-garment printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-to-garment_printing)

**Strengths.** No setup cost — economical from exactly 1 unit (the engine of all print-on-demand). Unlimited colours, photographic detail, gradients.

**Weaknesses.** Thin ink layer: large solid areas on dark fabric look less dense than screen print (white underbase helps but doesn't equal it). Fades faster in the wash. Print area limited by the machine's platen — typically ~30×40 cm, the exact limitation that disqualified TPOP for our full-front designs. Needs high-cotton fabric.

**For BLH*DES.** Fine for one-off samples and the POD fallback route (René Bassett, Printify). Not the endgame for full-front statement pieces.

## 3. DTF — Direct-to-film

**How it works.** The design is inkjet-printed onto a release film, dusted with powdered hot-melt adhesive, then heat-pressed onto the garment. A modern evolution of transfer printing; no dedicated Wikipedia article yet — covered under the general umbrella of [textile printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_printing) and applied with a [heat press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_press).

**Strengths.** Vivid colour on any fabric or colour, no pre-treatment, large formats, viable from 1 unit. More flexible than DTG about garment material; many studios will press onto customer-supplied garments.

**Weaknesses.** The print sits *on* the fabric as a thin membrane — a sticker-like hand, can feel plasticky over large areas; edges can crack/peel over time. Less "luxury" tactile quality than screen or DTG.

**For BLH*DES.** Useful for fast local prototyping in Barcelona (DTF studios are the most flexible about "prenda propia"). Not for the sold product.

## 4. Heat transfer vinyl (HTV)

**How it works.** Coloured vinyl sheets are cut to shape (plotter), weeded, and heat-pressed onto the garment. → [Heat transfer vinyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer_vinyl)

**Strengths.** Cheap for simple 1-colour shapes, numbers, names; very durable.

**Weaknesses.** Solid plastic film hand; no detail, no gradients, size limits. Sports-jersey aesthetics.

**For BLH*DES.** Not suitable — listed for completeness (many Barcelona shops quote it by default for small runs).

## 5. Dye sublimation

**How it works.** Dye is printed on paper, then turned into gas under heat so it bonds *inside* the fibre — zero hand, photographic, all-over prints possible. → [Dye-sublimation printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dye-sublimation_printing)

**The catch.** Only bonds to **polyester** (and only light colours). On a 100% organic cotton black tee it simply doesn't work.

**For BLH*DES.** Chemically incompatible with the collection. Know it to refuse it.

## 6. Embroidery

**How it works.** The design is digitised into stitches and sewn by machine. → [Machine embroidery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_embroidery)

**Strengths.** The most premium-reading decoration per cm²; effectively permanent; texture and relief.

**Weaknesses.** Priced per stitch-count → small marks only; can't do large areas on jersey (puckering); one-time digitisation fee.

**For BLH*DES.** Future option for a small ✻ chest/sleeve mark on later drops — not for the big front prints.

## 7. Pad printing (tampography)

**How it works.** A silicone pad picks ink from an etched plate and stamps it onto (usually small or curved) surfaces. → [Pad printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pad_printing)

**For BLH*DES.** Apparel use is mostly **inside neck labels** (printed directly on fabric instead of a sewn tag). Relevant when we get to custom labelling.

## Comparison at a glance

| Method | Min. viable qty | Ink density on black | Durability | Max size | Hand feel |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Screen print | ~10+ | ★★★★★ | ★★★★★ | Biggest | Slight raise (premium) |
| DTG | 1 | ★★★ | ★★★ | ~30×40 cm platen | Soft, in-fabric |
| DTF | 1 | ★★★★ | ★★★ | Large | Membrane/sticker |
| HTV | 1 | ★★★★ | ★★★★ | Small shapes | Plastic film |
| Sublimation | 1 | n/a on cotton | ★★★★★ | All-over | None |
| Embroidery | 1 | n/a | ★★★★★ | Small marks | Raised stitching |

## Appendix — fabric & finishing vocabulary

The garment side of the same conversations (full detail in [[Projects/Blhades/production-plan|Production Plan]]):

- **gsm** (grams per square metre) — fabric weight; see [grammage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammage) and [units of textile measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_textile_measurement). 240 = premium streetwear standard; 300+ = the luxury push.
- **[Open-end spinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-end_spinning)** — fast, cheap yarn; dry/fuzzy hand (the Stanley/Stella Freestyler's deliberate character).
- **[Ring spinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_spinning)** — aligned, twisted fibres; smoother and stronger.
- **[Combing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combing)** — removes short fibres before spinning; "combed ring-spun" is the luxury floor.
- **[Jersey (fabric)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_(fabric))** — the single-knit construction all these tees use.
- **[Organic cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_cotton)** / **[GOTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Organic_Textile_Standard)** — the certification behind "organic" claims.
- **[Dyeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyeing)** (garment dyeing) + enzyme wash (see [cellulase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulase)) + silicone softeners — the finishing stack behind the "expensive hand".
